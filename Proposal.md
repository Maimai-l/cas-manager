# CAS Proposal Writing Skill

## Purpose
Produce a clean, compliant CAS experience proposal in plain text, following the format described by the student's school CAS handbook. The output should be ready to copy into ManageBac or submit to a CAS advisor with minimal extra work from the student.

---

## Core Writing Principles

### The "Minimum Work" Rule
This is the most important principle. Every goal, evidence requirement, and evaluation criterion must be achievable through the most effortless means possible — typically a photograph taken during the session. Never write goals that require:
- Surveys or questionnaires
- Numerical targets that need tracking (e.g. "10 kilograms", "75% of members")
- Separate write-ups or reports beyond what ManageBac reflections already require
- Actions outside of the scheduled sessions

When in doubt, default to: "Evidence: Photographs taken during sessions."

### Keep It Vague but Credible
Goals should sound purposeful and specific enough to be approved, but broad enough that they are almost impossible to fail. Avoid any language that creates a hard pass/fail threshold the student must actively prove.

Bad: "To recycle at least 10 kilograms of waste by December, verified by a weigh-in log."
Good: "To contribute to the production of usable compost through regular participation in sessions."

### No Irrelevant Padding
Do not connect the experience to the student's academic subjects, future career, or unrelated personal traits unless the student explicitly requests it. Keep the Personal Value section grounded in the experience itself.

### Natural, Unawkward Social Goals
If a personal goal involves social interaction or communication, phrase it in terms of engagement and collaboration — never in terms of "making friends" or relationship outcomes, which reads as awkward in a formal proposal. Use language like "engage meaningfully with peers" or "strengthen communication skills through collaborative work."

## Required Sections and How to Write Each

### 1. Header Block
Include as plain text (no table):
- Experience Title
- CAS Strand(s)
- Supervisor (name and role)
- Location
- Duration (start date, frequency, end point, session length)

### 2. Description and Overall Aims
2–3 paragraphs. Cover:
- What the student will actually do, concretely
- Why it matters / what need it addresses
- Which CAS strand it primarily fits and why
- Brief mention of secondary benefits (e.g. collaboration, skill development)

Do not begin with "I". Start with the experience or activity as the subject.

### 3. Experience Goals (2 goals)
SMART in spirit, but vague on hard metrics. Each goal has:
- A one-sentence goal statement
- "Evidence: Photographs taken during sessions." (default, unless context clearly calls for something else)

### 4. Personal Goals (2 goals)
Same format. Focus on:
- A skill or knowledge area the student wants to develop
- A social/interpersonal dimension (phrased without awkwardness — see above)

### 5. Personal Value
1 short paragraph. Connect to:
- Personal motivation (why this matters to the student)
- Real-world applicability or transferable skills
- 1–2 relevant UN SDGs if they fit naturally (do not force this)

### 6. Planning
1 paragraph. Cover:
- Whether prior knowledge is needed (usually: no)
- A rough phase structure (3 phases works well for semester-length experiences)
- The supervisor's role

### 7. Timeframe
Short paragraph or 3–4 sentences. State:
- Start date
- Frequency and day/time
- Approximate session length
- How sessions are structured across the experience

### 8. Evaluation
1 short paragraph. Keep criteria soft and photo-evidence-based. The student should "consider the experience successful if [observable outcome]." Avoid checklists or numbered criteria.

### 9. Learning Outcomes (maximum 3)
Select the 3 most naturally supported by the experience. Write 2–3 sentences per LO explaining how the experience demonstrates it. Do not list all 7. Prioritise:
- LO2 (new skills) — almost always applicable
- LO5 (collaboration) — applicable to any group experience
- LO6 (global engagement) — applicable to any environmental or community service experience
- LO4 (commitment) — applicable to long-term experiences
- LO7 (ethics) — applicable when the experience involves an ethical choice (e.g. recycling instead of landfill)

---

## Formatting Rules
- Plain text only. No bold, no headers with # symbols in the final output — use ALL CAPS section titles separated by blank lines and dashes.
- Write in formal but natural English. Avoid corporate jargon.
- Paragraphs, not bullet points (except within Learning Outcomes where brief explanation follows the LO label).
- Do not use em-dashes mid-sentence excessively.

---

## Input Handling

### If given two draft documents to merge:
Extract the best elements from each — typically the cleaner structure from one and the more specific details from the other. Do not simply concatenate. Rewrite from scratch using the merged content.

### If given raw notes or a single draft:
Identify which sections are present and which are missing. Fill gaps using reasonable inference from context. Flag anything that genuinely requires the student to confirm (e.g. supervisor name, exact start date).

### If given a completed proposal to improve:
Apply the Minimum Work Rule and tone corrections. Do not restructure unless the format is significantly wrong.

---

## Common Mistakes to Avoid
- Writing evidence requirements that go beyond photos (surveys, logs, weigh-ins)
- Phrasing social goals as "making friends" or "building friendships"
- Adding filler about academic subjects or future studies unless requested
- Addressing more than 3 Learning Outcomes
- Using specific numbers in goals that the student must actively track and prove
- Starting the Description section with "I"
